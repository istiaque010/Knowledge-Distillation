Knowledge Distillation of a Small Autoregressive Language Model

Teaching a smaller model to write stories by copying a bigger one

Project 2 tutorial

In Project 1 we built a 13.9-million-parameter story-writing model from scratch. Here it becomes the teacher. We train a model 2.6× smaller, the student, to copy it, and measure what the student keeps and what it loses. Everything runs on a normal computer's CPU.

This page explains every step: what we did, why, what result we got, and how that result told us what to do next. You don't need to read Project 1 first. Idea 1 recaps it.

The one question

How much can the model be compressed through knowledge distillation while keeping its text-generation quality?

The one idea behind everything

Normally a model learns only "the next word was girl". In distillation, the student also learns what the teacher thought:

Real text says:     next word = "girl"                                (one answer)
Teacher thinks:     girl 68% · boy 19% · bird 2% · bunny 2% · …          (a full opinion)
Student learns:     to match the teacher's whole opinion, not just the one answer

The pipeline (the order we did things in)

Project 1 teacher→ Check & freeze it→ Design a student→ Student without teacher→ Distillation loss→ Distilled student→ Try other settings→ Measure quality & speed→ Read the stories→ Final answer

Idea 1 What Project 1 built (the teacher)

A program that guesses the next piece of text, and gives a chance to every possible guess.

Project 1 built a language model: a Transformer (GPT-style) with 6 layers and 13.9 million parameters (numbers that training adjusts). It reads text cut into tokens (words or pieces of words; it knows 8,192 of them) and, at every position, outputs a probability for each of the 8,192 tokens. To write a story it picks a token, adds it, and guesses again.

It was trained for 9.9 hours on 1.77 million children's stories (TinyStories) and scores a test perplexity of 5.74. Roughly, it is choosing between about 6 likely tokens at each step. Lower is better.

an experienced storyteller who has read 1.7 million children's books. Ask "Once upon a time, there was a little…" and they will say "girl, probably, or maybe boy".

Idea 2 What is knowledge distillation?

A small model (student) learns by copying a big trained model (teacher).

A big model is accurate but slow and heavy. A small model is fast and light but, trained on its own, less accurate. Knowledge distillation (KD) trains the small model to copy the big model's outputs. The aim is to get most of the big model's quality at the small model's cost.

  • The teacher is already trained and is frozen: it never changes. It only gives answers.
  • The student has fewer parameters. It is the only model that learns.
  • After training, you throw the teacher away and use only the student.

an apprentice learning from a master chef. The apprentice can't memorise every dish the master has ever cooked, but by tasting the master's dishes and copying the master's judgement, they learn much faster than from a recipe book alone.

Idea 3 Hard labels and soft targets

One right answer, or a ranked opinion about every answer?

A hard label is what the real text says: the next word was "girl". It is 100% "girl", and every other word counts as equally wrong. "Boy" (a fine answer) is punished as much as "refrigerator" (a silly one).

A soft target is the teacher's full probability list. It says "girl" is best, "boy" is close, "bird" is possible, and "refrigerator" is nearly impossible. This extra information about the wrong answers is sometimes called dark knowledge.

Hard label (from the real text)
Soft target (from the teacher)

With hard labels, one training example teaches one fact. With soft targets, it teaches how similar thousands of words are to each other. That is why a student can learn faster from a teacher than from the raw text.

Idea 4 Softmax and temperature (T)

Turning up the temperature makes the teacher's opinion easier to read.

The model first gives every token a raw score called a logit. Softmax turns the scores into probabilities that add up to 100%. A trained teacher is very confident, so most of the probability sits on 1–2 tokens and the rest are tiny numbers. The useful "dark knowledge" is hidden in those tiny numbers.

The temperature T divides the scores before softmax. T = 1 is normal. T > 1 flattens the distribution, so the small probabilities grow and the student can see them.

p(token) = exp(score / T) / sum of exp(all scores / T)
The teacher's real probabilities (Phase 6). Pick a prompt and a temperature.
At T = 1, only 5% of the probability lies outside the top 10 tokens. At T = 2 it is 78%, spread over the whole vocabulary. That is what the student gets to learn from.

a teacher who whispers. At T = 1 you can only hear their favourite answer. Turning up T is like turning up the volume: now you hear their second, third and hundredth choice too.

Idea 5 Two ways to measure "how wrong": cross-entropy and KL divergence

One compares the student with the real text, the other compares it with the teacher.

Cross-entropy (CE) is the normal language-model loss from Project 1. It looks only at the probability the student gave the correct token: CE = −log p(correct token). If the student gives "girl" 50%, CE = 0.69; at 90%, CE = 0.11. Lower is better.

KL divergence measures how different two whole probability lists are. KL(teacher ‖ student) is 0 when the student's list is exactly the teacher's, and grows as they differ. It is never negative. In this project, it is also our main measure of how close a student is to the teacher.

Idea 6 The distillation loss: α, T and T²

Mix both losses, and choose how much each one counts.

Loss = α · CE(student, real token)  +  (1 − α) · T² · KL(teacher_T ‖ student_T)
  • α (alpha) sets the mix. α = 1 means "ignore the teacher" (normal training). α = 0.5 is half and half. α = 0.25 leans mostly on the teacher.
  • T is the temperature from Idea 4. Both teacher and student are softened by the same T.
  • T²: softening makes the KL part's pull on the student about T² times weaker. Multiplying by T² puts it back, so changing T doesn't secretly change the mix.
Real numbers: an untrained student on one real batch (Phase 7). Move α, pick T.
CE is 9.05 for every T (it doesn't use T). The KD part changes a lot with T. Without the T² factor, at T = 4 it would almost vanish (0.25).
def distillation_loss(student_logits, teacher_logits, targets, temperature=2.0, alpha=0.5):
    ce = F.cross_entropy(student_logits.reshape(-1, V), targets.reshape(-1))
    log_ps = F.log_softmax(student_logits / temperature, dim=-1)   # student, softened
    log_pt = F.log_softmax(teacher_logits / temperature, dim=-1)   # teacher, softened
    kd = F.kl_div(log_ps, log_pt, log_target=True, reduction="batchmean")
    return alpha * ce + (1 - alpha) * temperature**2 * kd          # (file: kd/distillation.py)

Idea 7 Freezing the teacher

The teacher must give the same answer every time, and must never learn.

During one training step the same text goes into both models. Only the student is updated:

text ─→ teacher (frozen, no gradient) ─→ teacher probabilities ─┐
     └→ student                       ─→ student probabilities ─┴→ distillation loss ─→ update the STUDENT only

"Frozen" means four things, and Project 2 does all four:

  • Eval mode: turns dropout off. Dropout randomly switches off parts of the model during training; left on, the teacher's answers would change randomly.
  • requires_grad = False: the teacher's weights can't be changed by training.
  • torch.no_grad(): PyTorch doesn't even record the teacher's maths for training, which also saves memory and time.
  • Hash check: the file teacher.pt is compared with Project 1's fingerprint list before use, so we know it is exactly the teacher that scored 5.74.
Why it matters: in Phase 3 we broke it on purpose. With dropout on, the teacher's answers to the same text differed by up to 6.4 in score. With gradients on, the student's loss reached all 76 of the teacher's weight tensors. Neither mistake gives an error message.

Idea 8 The baseline and a fair test

"The student scored 8.59" means nothing unless you know what it would score without the teacher.

The baseline is the same student, trained the normal way (α = 1, no teacher). The experiment is student with teacher vs student without teacher. Everything else is kept identical:

  • the same size (4 layers, 5.3M parameters) and the same code;
  • the same data in the same order (same random seed, 1337);
  • the same number of steps (3,000), batch, learning-rate schedule and optimizer;
  • the same tests: the same test stories, prompts, seeds and error rules.

Only the loss differs. So any difference in the result is caused by the teacher.

testing a new fertiliser. You plant two identical seeds in identical pots with the same water and light, and give the fertiliser to only one. Otherwise you can't tell what made the plant grow.

Idea 9 "Gap closed": one number for how much distillation helped

The teacher is the goal and the baseline is the start. How far did distillation move the student?

gap closed = (PPL_baseline − PPL_distilled) / (PPL_baseline − PPL_teacher)
Test perplexity (lower is better): real results
(9.63 − 8.59) / (9.63 − 5.74) = 26.7% of the gap closed, with no extra parameters and no extra data. With the best setting found (α = 0.25, perplexity 8.43) it is 30.7%.

Idea 10 Compression and speed

What do we gain by being small?

  • Compression ratio = teacher parameters ÷ student parameters = 13.89M ÷ 5.32M = 2.61×.
  • Latency: seconds to write a 200-token story. Speedup = teacher latency ÷ student latency.
  • Tokens per second: how many tokens the model writes (or scores) each second.
  • Peak RAM: the most memory the program uses. On a GPU this would be "GPU memory".

Writing one token at a time has a fixed overhead (Python, sampling, starting many small calculations), so a 2.6× smaller model is "only" about 1.8× faster at writing. When scoring lots of text at once, the CPU does real maths and the student is 2.4–2.8× faster, close to its size ratio.

The Project 1 decisions that made Project 2 possible

  • A clean, fixed test set that was never used for choices. Without it, "gap closed" would be meaningless.
  • A known-good decoding setting (top-p 0.95) and fixed seeds, so all models write stories under identical conditions.
  • Written-down limits (88% of the teacher's stories have a contradiction). A student can't be expected to beat its teacher, so these set honest expectations.
  • A baseline of the same shape (4 layers, width 256) already tried in Project 1 (perplexity 9.68 on 100K stories). Project 2's student uses the same shape.
Rule: Project 2 never writes anything into project1/. If the teacher changed, every comparison would be broken.

Error analysis

Which skills were lost when the teacher was compressed?

What and why

60 stories per model, labelled by hand with Project 1's strict rules (one slip anywhere counts):

  • Contradiction: something clashes with an earlier fact or doesn't follow from it.
  • Grammar: at least one broken sentence or made-up word.
  • Entity consistency: a character changes name, gender or species, or the wrong character speaks.

To avoid bias, the two students' 120 stories were shuffled together under hidden IDs and labelled blind. To check that today's labels match Project 1's, 20 teacher stories were re-labelled: 14–16 of 16 agreed.

  • Lost first and most: story-level consistency. All 120 student stories contradict themselves (a key becomes a necklace, then a "bracelet with teeth"). Distillation doesn't fix this.
  • Keeping characters apart ("The cat thanked the cat") and grammar: distillation helps a little. With 60 stories the differences are not statistically significant (p ≈ 0.15–0.18), so they are a hint, not proof.
  • Copied habits: the distilled student repeats phrases as often as the teacher. Repetition rises with the teacher's weight (4 → 8 → 12 → 15 flagged stories as α goes 1 → 0.75 → 0.5 → 0.25). A student inherits its teacher's flaws.

Result: the answer

The model can be compressed 2.6× (13.9M → 5.3M parameters) and made ~1.8× faster at writing. Distillation makes that small model clearly better than training it alone. It closes 26.7% of the perplexity gap to the teacher (30.7% with the best setting), and it writes in the teacher's style. But the skill that needs the most capacity, keeping a whole story consistent, is lost in compression, and distillation does not bring it back.

Limits: one run per setting; every student had 3,000 steps (30% of the teacher's training), so longer training would improve all of them; one person labelled the errors; CPU only.

How we decided we were done

Success was defined in advance as "the distilled student keeps clearly more of the teacher's ability than a same-size student trained alone, while being cheaper to run". Both parts hold. Project 2 is finished.

Lessons learned (useful for any ML project)

  • A teacher must be finished and fixed. Project 1's packaging (weights, tokenizer, code, test set, hashes) made every comparison possible.
  • Build the baseline first, with the same budget. "8.59" is only meaningful next to "9.63".
  • Freeze, then test that it's frozen, and break it on purpose. These bugs make no noise.
  • Measure the cost before you train. Phase 4's 30-second benchmark predicted each 2-hour run to within a minute.
  • Fix the main setting in advance. Report the best setting found as an ablation, not as the headline.
  • Read the outputs, and label them blind. Perplexity can't tell you that every story contradicts itself.
  • A student copies everything, including the teacher's bad habits (repetition).

Glossary

From Project 1
Language model
a program that gives a probability to every possible next token.
Token / tokenizer
a piece of text with an ID number / the tool that cuts text into tokens (8,192 here).
Parameter
one number inside the model that training adjusts. Teacher 13.9M, student 5.3M.
Transformer, layer, head, width
the model design; layers are stacked blocks, heads are parallel attention parts, width is the length of each token's number list (384 teacher, 256 student).
Logits
the raw scores a model gives each token before softmax.
Softmax
turns scores into probabilities that add up to 100%.
Loss / cross-entropy (CE)
how wrong the guess of the real next token was: −log p(correct). Lower is better.
Perplexity (PPL)
eloss: about how many tokens the model is choosing between. Lower is better.
Train / validation / test
data to learn from / to check progress / for the final score only.
Checkpoint
a saved copy of a model's parameters.
Dropout
randomly switching off parts of a model during training to reduce memorising; off in eval mode.
Top-p sampling
writing by picking only from the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p (0.95 here).
Distinct-2
share of different word pairs in a story: a measure of variety.
New in Project 2
Knowledge distillation (KD)
training a small model (student) to copy the outputs of a big trained model (teacher).
Teacher / student
the big, frozen, trained model / the small model that learns from it.
Hard label
the single real next token from the text.
Soft target
the teacher's full probability list over all tokens.
Dark knowledge
the information in the teacher's small probabilities for "wrong" tokens (which ones are nearly right).
Temperature (T)
divides the logits before softmax; T > 1 flattens the probabilities so the small ones show.
KL divergence
how different two probability lists are; 0 means identical. Our measure of closeness to the teacher.
α (alpha)
the weight of the real-data loss in the mix; 1 − α goes to copying the teacher.
T² factor
multiplies the KD part to keep its strength the same whatever T is.
Frozen
eval mode + no gradients + no_grad: the teacher can't change and always gives the same answer.
Gradient
the direction to nudge each parameter to lower the loss; only the student's are used.
Hash (SHA-256)
a fingerprint of a file; if one byte changes, the fingerprint changes.
Baseline
the same student trained without the teacher: the score to beat.
Ablation
an experiment that changes one setting to see how much it matters.
Gap closed
(PPL_baseline − PPL_distilled) / (PPL_baseline − PPL_teacher): how far distillation moved the student toward the teacher.
Top-1 agreement
how often the student's most likely token is the same as the teacher's.
Compression ratio
teacher parameters ÷ student parameters (2.61×).
Latency / speedup
time to write a 200-token story / teacher latency ÷ student latency.
Blind labelling
judging outputs without knowing which model wrote them, to avoid bias.
Statistically significant
a difference too large to be explained by chance; with 60 stories, small differences often aren't.
